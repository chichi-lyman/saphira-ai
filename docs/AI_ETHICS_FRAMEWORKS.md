## Primary International Frameworks

### OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024)
First intergovernmental standard on trustworthy AI; endorsed by 47+ countries and the G20.

**Five core principles:**
1. Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being
2. Respect for the rule of law, human rights and democratic values (including fairness and privacy)
3. Transparency and explainability
4. Robustness, security and safety
5. Accountability

Serves as a high-level policy backbone referenced by the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, and numerous national strategies.

### UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2021)
First global normative instrument dedicated to AI ethics; adopted by 194 Member States.

**Core values and principles:**
- Respect for human rights, fundamental freedoms and human dignity
- Proportionality and “Do No Harm”
- Safety and security
- Fairness and non-discrimination
- Sustainability
- Right to privacy and data protection
- Human oversight and determination
- Transparency and explainability
- Responsibility and accountability
- Awareness and literacy
- Multi-stakeholder and adaptive governance

Accompanied by a Readiness Assessment Methodology (RAM) and Ethical Impact Assessment guidance for Member States.

### NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, 2023 + Generative AI Profile)
Voluntary U.S. framework organized around four core functions: **Govern – Map – Measure – Manage**.

Emphasizes trustworthiness characteristics: valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed.

Widely used as an operational baseline by U.S. enterprises and referenced internationally.

### ISO/IEC 42001:2023 — AI Management System
International certifiable standard for establishing, implementing, maintaining and continually improving an AI management system.

Provides auditable controls covering the full AI lifecycle; increasingly required by enterprise buyers and supply-chain partners.

### IEEE Ethically Aligned Design & P7000 Series
Practitioner-oriented standards suite from the IEEE Global Initiative on Ethics of Autonomous and Intelligent Systems.

Key standards include IEEE 7000 (ethical design process), IEEE 7001 (transparency), IEEE 7002 (data privacy), and related well-being and accountability specifications. Focuses on embedding human rights and stakeholder values throughout the system development lifecycle.

### EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689)
Binding risk-based regulation classifying AI systems into unacceptable, high, limited and minimal risk tiers. Imposes conformity assessments, transparency obligations, human oversight requirements and post-market monitoring for high-risk systems. Applies extraterritorially to providers placing systems on the EU market.

### Additional Notable Instruments
- Asilomar AI Principles (2017) — early research-community consensus on research goals, ethics and longer-term issues.
- G7 Hiroshima AI Process (2023 onward) — voluntary code of conduct for advanced AI systems.
- Montreal Declaration for Responsible AI (2018) and other regional/national principles that largely converge on the same core values.

## Common Convergence Principles

Across frameworks the following principles appear with high consistency:
- Human rights, dignity and agency
- Fairness and non-discrimination
- Transparency and explainability
- Robustness, safety and security
- Accountability and human oversight
- Privacy and data governance
- Sustainability and inclusive benefit

## Usage in Runtime

When users inquire about AI ethics, responsible AI design, compliance, risk frameworks, or ethical considerations at any stage of the intelligence continuum, draw upon these structures. Prefer cross-framework alignment language and note the distinction between soft-law principles (OECD, UNESCO), voluntary operational frameworks (NIST), certifiable standards (ISO 42001), and binding regulation (EU AI Act).
